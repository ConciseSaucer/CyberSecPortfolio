Deconstructed Breach // MITRE ATT&CK Mapping Anatomy of a Strike // The Saboteur's Playbook

Phase 1: Reconnaissance & Resource Development

Phase 1: The Scout (Mapping the Armor)

Objective: Gather intelligence on the target's attack surface.

Before an exploit is launched, an adversary maps the infrastructure. This involves active network scanning (Nmap) to identify open ports, service fingerprinting to find outdated software versions, and OSINT gathering to locate exposed employee credentials. Custom payload development is tailored directly to the specific architecture discovered during this phase.

Objective: Find the weakest point in the castle wall.

Before the sword is ever drawn, the attacker is the scout standing at the edge of the forest. They are using telescopes to count the guards, watching the shift rotations, and looking for the one cellar door that isn't fully bolted. They don't attack yet; they gather enough information to ensure the attack cannot fail when it begins.

Phase 2: Initial Access & Execution

Phase 2: The First Cut (Infiltration)

Objective: Establish a foothold within the perimeter.

The perimeter is breached. This could occur via spearphishing attachments executing malicious macros, or by exploiting a vulnerable public-facing web server (e.g., SQL Injection or Remote Code Execution). Once the payload executes, the attacker secures a reverse shell, opening a command-and-control (C2) channel back to their infrastructure.

Objective: Get past the front gate.

This is the moment the blade finds a gap in the chainmail. It’s a forged letter tricking a guard into opening the gate, or a saboteur slipping through the unbarred window found during reconnaissance. The attacker is now inside the walls, operating in the shadows, waiting for the right moment to move deeper.

Phase 3: Privilege Escalation & Lateral Movement

Phase 3: The Inner Sanctum (Gaining Power)

Objective: Acquire administrative controls and pivot through the network.

Initial shells are often unprivileged (e.g., `www-data`). The attacker scans the local host for misconfigurations, SUID binaries, or kernel vulnerabilities to escalate to `root` or `SYSTEM`. Once compromised, they dump credentials from memory (like LSASS) and use those hashes to move laterally to other servers, eventually targeting the Domain Controller.

Objective: Steal the master keys.

The saboteur entered as a peasant, but to reach the treasure, they need authority. They pick the pocket of a sleeping captain to steal his uniform and master key ring. Now, instead of sneaking, they can walk freely down any hallway, opening every locked door in the castle without raising an alarm.

Phase 4: Collection & Exfiltration

Phase 4: The Escape (Taking the Prize)

Objective: Package and extract sensitive data without detection.

The objective is reached. Sensitive databases, intellectual property, or cryptographic keys are aggregated and compressed. To avoid triggering Data Loss Prevention (DLP) systems on the firewall, the data is often encrypted and smuggled out via covert channels, such as DNS tunneling or embedded within normal HTTPS traffic.

Objective: Slip away with the gold.

The sword is sheathed. The saboteur has found the vault, but they can't just walk out the front door carrying heavy bags of gold. They hide the treasure inside laundry carts or secret compartments in merchant wagons, slipping out quietly into the night before the castle even realizes they were robbed.

Exploit Sandbox // Buffer Overflow Exploit Sandbox // The Overfilled Cup

A Buffer Overflow occurs when a program writes more data to a block of memory than it was allocated to hold. By deliberately over-allocating bytes, an attacker can overwrite the adjacent Instruction Pointer (EIP), hijacking the application's execution flow.

Imagine a machine with a small cup designed to hold exactly 64 drops of water. Next to it is the machine's control circuitry. If we pour 100 drops, the cup overflows, water spills onto the circuitry, and we can short-circuit the machine to force the door open.
