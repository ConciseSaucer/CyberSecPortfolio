Vulnerability Mitigation: Brute Force vs. Dictionary
Systems administration requires designing authentication pipelines resilient to mass compromise. A brute force attack relies on sheer GPU computational throughput to iterate through keyspaces. Conversely, a dictionary attack exploits low-entropy human behavior by hashing pre-computed lists (Rainbow Tables) to achieve high-volume breaches instantly.

The Solution: Salts & Key Stretching
To mitigate this, modern architectures do not store raw hashes. We utilize Argon2id, introducing a per-user cryptographically secure pseudorandom number (the Salt) appended to the string. This destroys the utility of Rainbow Tables. We then configure high iteration counts (Key Stretching) to ensure the hashing process is intentionally latency-heavy, mathematically exhausting any hardware attempting an offline brute force.

The Threat: Master Keys vs. Brute Force
Think of a brute force attack as someone trying to kick down a front door—it’s loud, slow, and requires massive physical effort. A dictionary attack, however, is like a thief with a ring of "Master Keys." Instead of breaking the door, they try 10,000 common keys they've found elsewhere, hoping your lock is one of the standard ones that matches their pre-cut list.

The Solution: The Custom Lock (Salting)
To stop the thief, we "Salt" the password. Salting is like a Blacksmith forging a completely unique lock cylinder for every single door. Even if the thief steals a Master Key list that works for a million other houses, those keys are physically incompatible with your custom cylinder. Your lock effectively speaks a language the thief's keys don't understand.

Exhausting the Attacker: "The Sticky Mechanism" (Stretching)
Finally, we use Key Stretching to make the lock "sticky." It takes a legitimate resident one second to turn the key. For a person entering their home once a day, the delay is invisible. But for an intruder trying to rotate through 14 trillion keys rapidly, that one-second delay per attempt forces their "instant" attack to take hundreds of years to complete.
